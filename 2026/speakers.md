<article class="speaker-entry" id="AD">
  <h3>Victor Perez</h3>
  <p class="speaker-affiliation">Visual Effects Supervisor</p>
  <p class="talk-title">
    The Beauty of Imperfection: Human Craft in the Age of AI
  </p>

    
  <p>
    <strong>Abstract:</strong> As AI makes visual beauty instantly accessible, cinema enters a new
    creative era where production value and artistic value begin to
    separate. When images can be generated by anyone, the central question
    is no longer what can be shown, but why it should be shown, and, just
    as importantly, what should remain unseen.
      This talk explores how AI is shifting visual storytelling beyond
    spectacle and technical achievement toward meaning, authorship,
    restraint, and human imperfection. With a focus on cinematography and
    visual effects, it argues that the future of the image will not be
    defined by flawless beauty, but by craft: the deliberate act of shaping,
    selecting, withholding, and piecing beauty together into emotional and
    narrative meaning. Maybe we needed AI to remind us what makes us human.
  </p>

  <p><em>
    <strong>Biography:</strong> Victor Perez is a renowned figure in the global visual effects industry.
    His career spans over 28 years so far, and he has worked in many areas
    of visual storytelling. Starting as a photographer and cinematographer,
    extending his interest to the whole image post-production process and
    evolving into a visual effects artist with a special focus on
    photorealistic compositing of CGI. Along the way, he has worked on a
    number of Hollywood films, including Christopher Nolan’s ‘The Dark
    Knight Rises’, ‘Rogue One: A Star Wars Story’, ‘Harry Potter and the
    Deathly Hallows’, and ‘Pirates of the Caribbean: On Stranger Tides’,
    plus many more. Eventually, he became a Film Director, and his career
    naturally led him to his current role as a Visual Effects Supervisor.
    What really drives Perez is his focus on storytelling. He is fascinated
    by how new technologies can help filmmakers tell stories in ways never
    seen before. His work is a fine alchemy of Science, Technology, and Art,
    reflecting his passion for innovation and creativity.
  </em></p>
 
</article>

  <h3>Robin Kahlow</h3>
  <p class="speaker-affiliation">Runway</p>
  <p class="talk-title">Simulating the Stack</p>

  <p><strong>Abstract:</strong>
    Every pipeline that produces images, sound or interactive experiences
    is built from layers of explicit systems. Drawing on Runway's research
    into world models and real-time simulation, this keynote maps which of
    those layers learned simulation can handle today, where explicit systems
    still hold and what comes next.
  </p>

    explorable worlds. He led the research on Gen-Turbo, which brought
    high-quality video generation down from minutes to seconds, and helped
    build Runway's Gen series of image and video models, from pretraining
    to inference optimization.

<article class="speaker-entry" id="SP">
  <h3>Stuart Penn</h3>
  <p class="speaker-affiliation">
    VFX Supervisor at Framestore &middot; Production keynote
  </p>
  <p class="talk-title">Project Hail Mary: A VFX Journey</p>

  <p><strong>Abstract:</strong>
    Grounded in practical production, the visual effects for Project Hail Mary were built on a close collaboration with the filmmakers spanning from early pre-production through final delivery. Virtual Production was an essential tool for planning the shoot effectively. We will explore how Rocky was brought to life: a seamless integration of digital assets with an on-set practical puppet. A significant technical challenge was maintaining the transparent xenonite barrier separating Rocky and Grace. Executing the Zero-G sequences required digitally reconstructing costumes and sets to remove stunt rigs, while preserving the actor's original performance.

  </p>

  <p><em><strong>Biography:</strong>
    Stuart Penn is a Visual Effects Supervisor at Framestore in London,
    which he joined in 2000 after a decade as a research physicist. His
    scientific and artistic expertise has supported acclaimed productions
    including The Dark Knight, Guardians of the Galaxy, Gravity, Paddington 2
    and Avengers: Endgame. For Oscar-winning Gravity, he helped develop the
    techniques used to shoot its zero-gravity scenes, working with
    motion-control robots and the film's innovative LED lightbox. Having
    led Framestore's work on Bong Joon-ho's Mickey 17, he has put his
    scientific acumen to use on Phil Lord and Chris Miller's adaptation
    of Andy Weir's Project Hail Mary.
  </em></p>

<h2 id="industry-talks">Industry Talks</h2>


<article class="speaker-entry" id="daniele-siragusano">
  <h3>Daniele Siragusano</h3>
  <p class="speaker-affiliation">FilmLight</p>
  <p class="talk-title">
    How to train your Looks - Towards a Differentiable Colour Grading Pipeline
  </p>

  <p>
    <strong>Abstract:</strong> A motion-picture colour pipeline is a chain of image-processing
          stages assembled over several decades. Much of that chain
          predates the requirement of differentiability, and predates the
          availability of any smooth, expressive model of the creative look;
          looks were therefore captured empirically, by measuring film
          stocks, fitting colour models, constructing colour spaces and
          appearance models with branches and clamps, and freezing the
          result into a three-dimensional look-up table. The resulting
          pipeline is only marginally differentiable, and this property, rather
          than any absence of suitable methods, is the principal reason that
          machine learning, which depends on end-to-end gradients, has
          made limited progress in colour grading. We report an end-to-end
          differentiable colour pipeline. Its input transform is a linear
          matrix; its display rendering transform is a genuinely
          differentiable transform, reported separately; and its creative look
          stage, historically the final and most difficult component, is
          rendered differentiable by Chromogen, a smooth parametric look
          model, in combination with Chromogen ML, a method that
          recovers Chromogen parameters from an arbitrary existing look,
          including the baked look-up tables that encode the photochemical
          film look. The baked and the staged pipelines produce visually
          indistinguishable images, yet only the staged pipeline admits a
          gradient, and it is this property that restores the applicability of
          machine learning to colour grading.
  </p>

  <p><em>
    <strong>Biography:</strong> Daniele Siragusano is an image engineer at FilmLight. He leads the design of Truelight Colour Spaces, Baselight's colour management system. He also created the scene-referred grading tools BaseGrade, X Grade and Chromogen, and the Truelight CAM display rendering. His current work covers ML-driven grading, spectral imaging, film grain and visual adaptation in HDR.
  </em></p>


  


</article>

<article class="speaker-entry" id="russ-harding">
  <h3>Russ Harding</h3>
  <p class="speaker-affiliation">Partner and Director, Rhino Rock Studios</p>
  <p class="talk-title">
    From Screen to Game to Screen: How Immersive Is Becoming the Bridge
  </p>

  <p>
    <strong>Abstract:</strong> A single story world can now move between
    cinema, console, headset and phone. Drawing on a career spanning
    PlayStation's EyeToy and EyePet, Wonderbook: Book of Spells, and
    immersive experiences including Peaky Blinders: The King's Ransom,
    Infinite Inside and Project Hail Mary, Russ Harding explores how
    the camera learned to see the player and how film and TV worlds
    became playable. Looking ahead, he considers how real-time capture, AI and the latest
    headsets could carry audiences from screen to screen. The talk closes
    with an open discussion: who authors a world that lives everywhere,
    and how could research and production build that bridge together?
  </p>

  <p><em>
    <strong>Biography:</strong> Russ Harding is Partner and Director at
    Rhino Rock Studios, developing original IP, games and interactive
    experiences in collaboration with entertainment partners, alongside
    co-development and outsourcing services.
    With more than three decades in interactive entertainment, he has
    helped pioneer camera-based and augmented reality play, from
    PlayStation's EyeToy and EyePet to Wonderbook: Book of Spells, created
    in collaboration with J.K. Rowling. His credits also include Doctor
    Who: The Edge of Time, Peaky Blinders: The King's Ransom, Thief VR:
    Legacy of Shadow, the mixed reality adventure Infinite Inside, and
    Project Hail Mary: Journey Among the Stars.
    Through Rhino Rock Studios, Russ also advises studios and IP owners
    on interactive strategy, narrative and immersive production, and
    welcomes conversations about collaboration.
  </em></p>
</article>

<article class="speaker-entry" id="he-sun">
  <h3>He Sun</h3>
  <p class="speaker-affiliation">
    Milk Visual Effects and Royal Holloway, University of London
  </p>
  <p class="talk-title">
    From Technical Capability to Team Structure: Real-Time VFX in the
    Post-Production of High-End Fiction Television
  </p>

  <p>
    <strong>Abstract:</strong> Real-time game-engine technologies are
    moving from previsualisation and on-set virtual production (VP) into
    post-production visual effects (VFX) pipelines. Existing organisational
    research on VP concentrates on work at or before principal photography,
    and little is known about how a real-time team changes the division of
    labour in post-production, where most VFX labour remains. This paper
    reports a practitioner case study of Good Omens, Season 3 (2026) at
    Milk VFX, where a centralised five-artist Unreal Engine (UE) team and
    a conventional departmental pipeline operated concurrently over one
    shot list, with each shot routed according to its technical requirements.
    Drawing on production tracking, shot-level workflow breakdowns and
    the delivered artefacts, the paper maps each deployed real-time
    capability to the pipeline dependency it altered and to the
    organisational decision taken in response. Three findings emerge.
    First, the Unreal team absorbed asset, environment, animation, FX
    and lighting for the shots it owned. Second, the relationship between
    the Unreal and Compositing teams shifted from sequential hand-off to
    concurrent work on shared material. Third, specialist departments
    were retained, not replaced, for work the engine could not hold at
    delivery quality, so the facility ran two organisational forms over
    a single shot list. The paper argues that real-time capability is
    best treated as an input to organisational design, and that
    departmental specialisation becomes a deliberate choice rather than
    a default.
  </p>
</article>
